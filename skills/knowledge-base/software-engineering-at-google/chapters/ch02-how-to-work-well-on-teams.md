# Chapter 2: How to Work Well on Teams

## Core Idea

Software development is a team activity, even when the code is written by one person. Collaboration reduces single points of failure, catches problems earlier, and distributes knowledge; effective collaboration depends on humility, respect, trust, and deliberate handling of working-style differences.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The genius myth**: Treating software as the product of solitary heroes hides the collaboration and maintenance work required to sustain it.
  - When to use: Designing team norms, reviewing ownership, or evaluating “brilliant” but disruptive contributors.
  - How: Reward teaching, communication, and dependable teamwork alongside technical output.
- **The bus factor**: A project is fragile when too few people understand or can operate it.
  - When to use: Reviewing ownership, on-call readiness, and succession risk.
  - How: Share context through reviews, documentation, pairing, mentorship, and accessible code.
- **The three pillars of social interaction**: Humility, respect, and trust.
  - When to use: Feedback, disagreement, postmortems, and cross-team work.
  - How: Admit limits, assume colleagues are capable and well-intentioned, and create evidence that commitments and criticism are safe.
- **Blameless postmortems**: Analyze system conditions and decisions without turning the review into punishment.
  - When to use: Incidents, failed launches, and recurring team problems.
  - How: Reconstruct what happened, identify contributing conditions, and assign improvements rather than villains.

## Key Concepts

- **Isolation**: Working alone may feel efficient but increases blind spots and single-person dependency.
- **Psychological safety**: Confidence that questions, disagreement, and mistakes will not trigger humiliation or retaliation.
- **Feedback loop**: A short path from action to useful information and adjustment.
- **Working style**: A person’s preferred environment, communication pattern, and approach to focus and collaboration.
- **Brilliant jerk**: A high-output contributor whose behavior imposes large social and coordination costs.

## Mental Models

- Think of collaboration as risk reduction, not overhead.
- Optimize for the team’s pace of progress, not an individual’s local speed.
- Use disagreement to improve a decision; do not use it to establish status.
- Design offices and communication norms around varied needs instead of assuming one environment fits everyone.

## Anti-patterns

- **Hero ownership**: A single expert becomes the only person who can safely change or operate a system.
- **Genius exception**: Technical ability is used to excuse disrespect, poor communication, or knowledge hoarding.
- **Surprise criticism**: Feedback arrives only after a failure, when earlier course correction would have been cheaper.
- **Blame-centered postmortems**: People hide information next time, making the system less safe.

## Worked Example

The office case study contrasts engineers who need quiet focus with engineers who benefit from frequent interaction. The practical conclusion is not “open offices” or “private offices”; it is to recognize that forcing one working style can reduce productivity and collaboration. Teams should make communication easy while preserving places and norms for concentration.

## Key Takeaways

1. Share code and context early enough to reduce the bus factor.
2. Practice humility, respect, and trust explicitly; they are operating infrastructure.
3. Give and receive criticism as a tool for learning.
4. Treat team environment as an engineering design problem with trade-offs.
5. Use blameless analysis to improve systems and make failure information safe.
