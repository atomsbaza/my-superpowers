# Chapter 5: How to Lead a Team

## Core Idea

Leadership is service: create clarity, remove obstacles, develop people, and help the team deliver. A manager, tech lead, or tech-lead manager influences the system differently, but none should become a command bottleneck or a substitute for the team’s judgment.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Servant leadership**: Lead by making the team more capable and effective rather than accumulating control.
  - When to use: Team planning, conflict, hiring, and delivery problems.
  - How: Set direction, remove roadblocks, teach, give honest feedback, and delegate decisions to the people closest to the work.
- **Influence without authority**: Change outcomes through trust, evidence, framing, and relationships rather than formal power.
  - When to use: Cross-team dependencies or initiatives where you are not the manager.
  - How: Understand the other party’s goals, explain the shared benefit, make the desired action easy, and build credibility over time.
- **Positive leadership patterns**: Lose the ego, be a Zen master, be a catalyst, remove roadblocks, teach, set clear goals, be honest, and track team happiness.
  - When to use: Day-to-day leadership and team health.
  - How: Combine calm judgment, explicit expectations, honest feedback, and attention to motivation.

## Key Concepts

- **Engineering manager**: Owns team health, staffing, planning, execution, and growth.
- **Tech lead**: Provides technical direction and decision coordination.
- **Tech lead manager**: Combines management responsibility with technical leadership.
- **Servant leader**: A leader who increases team capability rather than directing every task.
- **Roadblock**: A constraint that prevents the team from making progress and needs leadership intervention.
- **Intrinsic motivation**: Motivation from meaningful work, mastery, autonomy, or purpose.

## Mental Models

- Think of a leader as a gardener: provide conditions for growth, remove weeds, and do not pull on the plants to make them grow faster.
- Delegate outcomes and context, not just tasks.
- A leader’s scarce resource is attention; use it where the team cannot act without you.
- “Hope is not a strategy”: name risks, decide, and create a feedback loop.

## Anti-patterns

- **Hire pushovers**: Selecting people who will never challenge the leader creates false agreement and weak decisions.
- **Ignore low performers or human issues**: Unaddressed problems consume the team’s attention and are unfair to everyone.
- **Be everyone’s friend**: Avoiding necessary feedback to preserve approval is not kindness.
- **Compromise the hiring bar**: Short-term staffing relief creates long-term coaching and quality costs.
- **Treat the team like children**: Excessive control suppresses ownership and judgment.

## Worked Example

In the influence-without-authority case, a technical leader must change behavior across a boundary without being able to order compliance. The effective path is to frame the problem in the other team’s terms, establish shared evidence, and offer a collaboration that removes their cost. The example demonstrates that persuasion is an engineering design problem: reduce friction and make the desired behavior rational.

## Key Takeaways

1. Choose leadership for service and influence, not status.
2. Make goals and decision ownership explicit.
3. Delegate so the team grows beyond the leader.
4. Address performance and human problems early and honestly.
5. Track happiness as a signal of team health, not as a popularity contest.
