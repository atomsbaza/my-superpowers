# Chapter 6: Leading at Scale

## Core Idea

Leadership at scale is the disciplined management of ambiguity, succession, and personal capacity. The three “Always” principles—Always Be Deciding, Always Be Leaving, and Always Be Scaling—turn a leader from a permanent bottleneck into a builder of self-driving systems.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Always Be Deciding**: Ambiguous problems require explicit trade-offs, a decision, and iteration—not a search for a timeless perfect answer.
  - When to use: Prioritization, architecture, staffing, and strategy.
  - How: Identify blinders, name the trade-offs, decide with current evidence, and revisit when conditions change.
- **Always Be Leaving**: Build a team that can solve the class of problems without your constant presence.
  - When to use: Succession, delegation, and scaling a leader’s span of influence.
  - How: Divide the problem space, develop leaders, transfer context and decision rights, and test whether the system works without you.
- **Always Be Scaling**: As success expands responsibility, proactively protect time, attention, and energy.
  - When to use: Growing teams, programs, or portfolios.
  - How: Separate important from urgent work, drop lower-value commitments, and invest in mechanisms that multiply effort.

## Key Concepts

- **Blinders**: Assumptions or missing perspectives that distort a decision.
- **Trade-off**: A choice that improves one dimension while accepting cost in another.
- **Self-driving team**: A team with enough context, ownership, and leadership to make progress without a central resolver.
- **Span of responsibility**: The amount of work and decision surface a leader must coordinate.
- **Energy budget**: The finite capacity for sustained attention and judgment.

## Mental Models

- Treat decisions as experiments with an explicit review condition.
- If you are the only person who can answer a recurring question, you own a scaling bug.
- Important work is not automatically urgent; protect strategic capacity.
- Learn to “drop balls” deliberately by choosing what not to carry.

## Anti-patterns

- **Decision paralysis**: Ambiguity is treated as a reason to wait for certainty.
- **Permanent centralization**: The leader keeps all context and approvals.
- **Heroic overcommitment**: Every successful project becomes another personal obligation.
- **Anchored team identity**: The team becomes attached to one leader’s solution instead of its mission.

## Worked Example

The web-search latency case illustrates the three principles together. A leader first identifies the competing constraints and decides which latency improvements matter most now. The problem is then subdivided into areas that other leaders can own, with progress measured and revisited. The leader scales by turning a one-off push into a portfolio of autonomous subproblems rather than personally coordinating every optimization.

## Key Takeaways

1. Make the best decision for current conditions, then iterate.
2. Make blind spots and trade-offs visible before committing.
3. Build successors and distribute decision rights.
4. Protect attention by choosing what to stop doing.
5. Measure leadership success by what works without the leader.
